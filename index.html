Dastarkhan 2036

A table, set ten years from now, for everyone who has ever been hungry for a place that was not on the map.

By Aziz · November 2026 · Duke Kunshan University

01 · What a machine can't dream 02 · What gets written into the past 03 · A day in 2036

01 How will I begin to dream something no machine can dream?

I begin with a smell. A model can list the ingredients of plov, but it has never stood in a kitchen at the exact second cumin hits hot oil and a whole apartment goes quiet because everyone knows what is coming. That second is where my dreams start: with homesickness, the one dataset nobody has scraped.

I study Data Science and Computer Science, so I know how a machine dreams: it averages. It looks at a million tables and hands back the most likely one. I want the least likely one. A table in Xi'an where a student from Tashkent, one from Almaty and one from Bishkek argue over whose lagman is correct, and all three are right.

I think in Russian, work in English and Uzbek, and am still earning my Chinese (西安 is the first city I want to get right). The dreams that matter live in the gaps between those languages. Dastarkhan does not translate to “table.” It is the cloth you unroll, and the promise that whoever sits down will be fed.

If I realize my hopes through AI, how do I keep it faithful to my will?

I treat AI as a sous-chef, never the chef. It may count, forecast and translate. It may not decide what deserves to be served.

My three kitchen rules for AI

House rules

  1. AI proposes, I taste. Models can forecast how much dough to make. Only a person decides what goes on the menu.
  2. No dish without a name. Every recipe credits the family, city or region it came from, so tradition is never flattened into “Asian fusion.”
  3. If I can't explain it, it doesn't enter the kitchen. A model that recommends something must show me why, in code I can read.

02 If memory is built from shared stories, which dreams do I want written into the past?

When 2036 looks back at 2026, I want the record to say that a Central Asian restaurant opened in Xi'an because students were tired of describing their food to people who had never tasted it. I want it remembered as “the place with the real non”, the address passed between first-years during their first homesick week.

Why Xi'an?

Xi'an was once Chang'an, the eastern end of the Silk Road. Opening there is not an import. It is a return.

What remains of me when all my stories are rewritten?

Recipes survive retelling better than names do. If everything else gets edited (my major, my city, my résumé), what should remain is a way of paying attention: noticing who is eating alone, and pulling out a chair.

03 What does an ordinary day in 2036 sound, look and move like?

Relationships, places, time, rhythm and movement, answered as one day.

  1. 05:30 Sound. The clack of the oven lid and bread slapped against hot clay. The day's rhythm is set by something rising before I do.
  2. 08:00 Movement. I cycle along the old city wall to the market, slowly, with the whole city laid out below me.
  3. 11:00 Time. Two quiet hours of Python. One small script predicts tonight's dough so nothing is thrown away. After that I only listen: my cooks' stories come first.
  4. 14:00 Relationships. Staff meal. The people who make my life meaningful are fed first, and I nurture them by listening before I plan.
  5. 19:00 Aesthetics. Low tables, indigo tile and patterned cloth, a room that came to look this way because the people who ate there kept bringing pieces of home to it.
  6. 23:00 Language. One notebook, four languages, switching mid-sentence the moment a word fits better somewhere else.
